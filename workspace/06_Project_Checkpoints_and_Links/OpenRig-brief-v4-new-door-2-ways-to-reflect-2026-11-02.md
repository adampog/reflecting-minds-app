Jake H. Davis reviewed the v3 walkthrough and the art department's character sheets.

**His calls.** These are recorded as R5 and R6 in the project's decision register. They are not
yet in a spec.

- **R5. Abstracted views work.** Back view and distance are fine. No faces for now.
- **R6. Keep *The well* and *Two births*;** both bring out real conflict. **Replace *The
  riverbank*.** It lacks emotional pull. What it reached for is still wanted: two groups who
  disagree about what counts as evidence for their position.

**His ideas for all scenarios.** These are suggestions, not decisions. This mockup is how Jake
and Alan will see them working before they decide.

1. Offer more than one way to reflect: attend to experience, put yourself in the other's shoes,
   perhaps others. Let people loop back and try another.
2. In group scenarios, let the user stand on one side, on the other, or in the middle as a
   neutral party trying to mediate.
3. *(Jake is undecided.)* In individual scenarios, let the user pick an initial leaning, then take
   that side, the opposite side, or the part of themselves mediating between two values they hold.
4. Develop each scenario's story a few sentences further, for emotional depth.

**Not for this version:** comparing the outcomes of different strategies (by users or by
researchers), live groups suggesting strategies to each other, and the identity-over-time
scenario (Jake's side note, marked not for action). Jake also restated the long-term aim: help
people see the structure of a disagreement without making consensus the required outcome. Nothing
here should work against that aim, and nothing here should try to build it.

## Door 2, Seeing things differently: the riverbank's replacement

What Jake asked for: two groups read the same evidence and disagree about what it shows, with
real emotional pull. In his words, "word these carefully so as not to take sides, but allow for
strong emotional reactions". There must be no hidden right answer.

There are three options. Alan picks one before this brief is queued, and the rig builds only that
one. The stories are drafts: refine them, but keep both sides at equal weight.

### Option A: *The inquiry* (recommended)

Jake's first example. It involves two groups, so it carries the stance choice. It is not a
medical scene, so it doesn't sit beside *Two births* as a second one.

> The town has opened an inquiry into its former harbour master. For twelve years they ran the
> port; last spring they lost the election to the people now on the council. The harbour
> accounts show a gap that nobody has explained. Half the town says that is exactly what an
> inquiry is for, and that refusing to look because of who it is would be its own kind of favour.
> The other half points out that the inquiry opened three weeks after the harbour master said they
> would stand again, that it was opened by the council they would stand against, and that the same
> accounts passed an audit two years ago. Both sides are reading the same ledger and the same
> calendar. The inquiry board meets at the end of the month.

The question it holds: what would show that this inquiry is fair, or that it isn't?

Take care with it. No parties, colours, flags, national institutions or real offices, and nothing
that places it in a real country. It must not read as a retelling of a real current case. If it
can't be told without that, report it rather than forcing it. Plate ideas: the harbour office at
dusk with one lit window, a ledger open on a table, two groups seen from behind at either end of
the quay.

### Option B: *The bedside*

Jake's second example, kept to the end-of-life case. The beginning-of-life version maps straight
onto a live political and religious dispute, which §6 rules out, so don't build it.

> Eight months after a fall, a person lies unresponsive in a hospital room. Their sibling visits
> every evening and plays the song the two of them sang as children. Twice, the fingers have
> moved, and the sibling is sure the person is still there. Their partner has sat through the same
> visits and seen the same scans. The doctors say the movement is a reflex and the scans show no
> awareness. The partner believes the person they loved has already gone, and remembers being told,
> years ago, "never keep me like that". The hospital has asked the family to agree on what happens
> next by the end of the month.

The question it holds: what counts as a sign that someone is still there?

This one has the strongest pull and the heaviest safety load. Put a plain one-line note before
entry that says what the scenario contains, and get a safety read before publication. Use no
religious markers, and make no medical claim that settles it. Plate ideas: a hospital window at
evening, an empty chair beside a bed, a corridor seen from a distance.

### Option C: *The last letter*

This is not one of Jake's examples, but he said he is open to others. It carries the lowest risk
of the three.

> When their grandmother died, the family found a letter in her handwriting leaving the house to
> the one grandchild who had moved in to care for her in her last two years. Half the family say
> it is her hand and her voice, and that the person who looked after her every day knew best what
> she wanted. The other half say that by then she was forgetting their names, and that the letter
> is dated the month the grandchild moved in. The house has to be kept or sold by spring.

The question it holds: what counts as evidence of what someone really wanted?

It has no political or religious markers. It brushes against Jake's identity-over-time note
(memory and decline) without building that scenario. Plate ideas: a house with one lit window, a
letter on a kitchen table, boxes half packed.

## Several ways to reflect (idea 1)

**Where it sits.** After the context (FR20) and, in group scenarios, after the stance choice. It
replaces the single attention practice at Notice.

**The screen:** "There's more than one way to look at this. Pick one. You can come back and try
another, or move on whenever you like."

| Strategy | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Notice what it stirs** | The existing attention practice: what you feel as you take this in, where you feel it, and how strong it is. No judging it. (Jake: attend to experience.) |
| **Stand where they stand** | Go through the situation as the other side, or the other value. What do they see, fear and want? Guesses about their motives are labelled as the user's own interpretation, as v3's Compare fix did. |
| **Map it** *(rig proposal)* | Set out what each side counts as evidence, what they agree on, and what can't be settled now. It is the smallest step toward the long-term aim. Build it only after the first two work, and label it in the viewer as a proposal. |

**The loop.** Each strategy ends back at the menu, which offers "Try another way" and "Move on".
"Move on" continues to Motivation and the remaining stages, as before.

**Rules.** The strategies get equal weight and plain names, and none is recommended. There are no
ticks, "tried" marks, counts or "2 of 3" anywhere (FR15). No strategy promises to help (AT17).
Nothing is compared, scored or summarised across strategies: outcome comparison belongs to a later
version, and the beta is not a research pilot. For each strategy, record how it maps onto the
seven stages. If one doesn't fit, report that rather than bending it.

## Take a side or mediate (ideas 2 and 3)

**Group scenarios** (*The well*, and the door-2 scenario). After the context and before the
reflection menu, ask: "Where would you like to stand?"

- With *[side one, in a plain phrase: "the families nearest the well"]*
- With *[side two: "the families at the far end of the path"]*
- Between them, trying to help both sides find a way through
- I'd rather not pick

The four get equal weight. The mediator is not framed as the wiser choice, and "rather not pick" is
a real option that costs nothing. Prompts after this point speak from the chosen place, for
example at Notice: "As one of the families nearest the well, what do you feel reading the
council's notice?" The authored text takes no side in any stance. The user can change where they
stand from the reflection menu. Build this on the fictional route only, leave the personal and
neutral routes unchanged, and say so in the viewer.

**Individual scenarios, as a switch** (Jake is undecided). This applies to *Two births* only. It
is off by default and labelled in the viewer "Option for review: Jake undecided".

- "Which way do you lean, if either?" The options are towards the family who asked first, towards
  the family with no one else, or I don't lean either way.
- Then: "Take that side", "Take the other side", or "Be the part of you that holds both". If the
  user doesn't lean, offer the first family, the second family, or the part that holds both.

The stance and the leaning are reflection content. Like everything else, they stay on the device
and are saved only through "Save on this device". They must not pre-empt FR18 in either direction.

## Deepened stories (idea 4)

Add a few sentences to each story so the stakes are felt, not only stated. Give each side matching
weight. Drafts follow, which the rig may refine.

***The well*** (added to the v3 text): "Near the well, the gardens are what most families will
eat through the winter, and one old couple there has nothing else. At the far end of the path, the
children already start each day carrying water, and by spring the walk will take two hours each
way. The council has met three times. Each time, someone from each side has said the same thing:
you are asking us to give up the one thing we cannot do without."

***Two births*** (added to the v3 text): "The first family booked her months ago. She promised to
be there, and the mother is frightened because her last birth went badly. The second message comes
an hour later from a farm across the valley: their own midwife is ill, the road to town is
flooded, and no one else can reach them tonight. Both labours have started. She can be at one."

## Pictures (R5)

- **No faces.** Figures may now appear only as abstracted views: from behind or at a distance,
  small in the frame, never the subject. This relaxes v3's "no bodies" on Jake's call (R5). The
  no-faces rule (FR02) stands.

  ethnicity, religion or politics.

  grouping and distance. The v3 character sheets made two surveyors look like one man and two

## Independent critique

The reviewing seat answers directly:

1. Does each way of reflecting stand on its own? Does coming back to the menu feel like an
   invitation or like a checklist? Is there any sign of counting, progress or completion?
2. In each stance, does the authored text take a side? Is the mediator shown as the better
   choice? Is "rather not pick" a real option?
3. The door-2 scenario: does it have the pull Jake asked for? Could it be read as a real current
   case, or does it carry a political or religious marker? Is either side easier to pick?
4. Did the added sentences bring depth without implying an outcome? In *The well*, does either
   need now read as the easier one to pick? (v3 left this open for the picture.)
5. Is there a face anywhere? Can any figure be read as a particular age, class, ethnicity or
   faith? Do two sides read as two groups?
6. The *Two births* leaning switch: does starting from a leaning push the user into defending it?
   Is "I don't lean either way" a real option?
7. Does the entry still feel remote or retreat-like to someone in difficulty?
8. What did *The well* cost by stage, and is the three-door estimate honest and labelled as an
   estimate?

Record disagreement between seats rather than resolving it silently.
